Frequently Asked Questions

How much smaller will my PDF get?
It depends almost entirely on what the file contains. Scanned documents and image-heavy brochures often drop by 50–90% because photographs compress well. A text-only PDF may barely change, since text is already stored very efficiently and there is little redundancy left to remove.
Will compression make my document look worse?
Text and vector graphics are unaffected — they stay perfectly sharp at any zoom level because they are not bitmap data. Only embedded images are recompressed, and at normal settings the difference is invisible on screen. If you are preparing a file for professional printing, keep the original as your master copy.
Why did my file barely shrink?
The PDF was probably already optimised, or it contains mostly text. Compression works by removing redundancy in image data and stripping unused objects, so a file that has already been through that process has little left to give. Files exported by modern software are often optimised by default.
Is compressing the same as reducing page size?
No. Compression reduces the file's byte size while keeping the page dimensions exactly the same. Your A4 pages stay A4. Nothing is cropped, scaled, or removed — the document looks the same, it just occupies less storage.
Can I compress a password-protected PDF?
Yes. A password prompt appears automatically after upload if the document is encrypted. Enter it and compression proceeds normally. You can also remove the password first with our Unlock PDF tool.
Are my files stored on your server?
No. Your PDF is processed in memory and the compressed result is streamed straight back to your browser. Nothing is written to disk, nothing is logged, and no copy is retained after your download completes.

Why PDFs Get So Large

Understanding the cause makes the result predictable. In almost every case the size comes from images. A scanned page is a photograph of paper, and at 300 dots per inch a single A4 sheet can occupy several megabytes before compression. Multiply that by forty pages and the file is enormous even though it contains nothing but text you could have typed in a minute.

The other contributors are smaller but add up. Embedded fonts carry every character in a typeface even when the document uses a handful. Metadata and revision history accumulate quietly each time a file is edited and re-saved. Duplicate objects appear when the same logo is inserted on every page as a separate copy rather than a shared reference.

What Compression Actually Does

Several optimisations run together. Embedded images are recompressed at a more efficient ratio, which is where the bulk of the saving comes from. Duplicate objects are detected and collapsed into a single shared reference. Unused resources left over from earlier edits are discarded. The internal cross-reference structure is rebuilt in a more compact form.

Crucially, text and vector graphics are never touched. They are stored as instructions rather than pixels, so they occupy very little space to begin with and remain perfectly sharp at any zoom level after compression.

What Results to Expect

Scanned documents compress the most dramatically, often shrinking by 70–90%, because photographic data has enormous redundancy. Image-heavy brochures and presentations typically drop by half or more. Text-only reports may only shrink by 5–15%, since there was never much to remove. Files already optimised by their originating software may barely change at all — that is not a failure, it means the work was already done.

If Compression Alone Is Not Enough

When the file is still too large, the answer is usually to remove content rather than compress harder. Remove Blank Pages strips the empty backs of double-sided scans, which in a duplex-scanned document can halve the page count immediately. Split PDF divides the document into parts that individually fit under your limit. Delete Pages removes sections the recipient does not need. Between them these usually solve what compression alone cannot.

Keep Your Original

Compression is not reversible — discarded image data cannot be recovered from the smaller file. Always keep the original as your master copy, especially for anything heading to professional print, and use the compressed version only for sharing and distribution.
