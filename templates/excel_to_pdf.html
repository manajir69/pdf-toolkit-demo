Frequently Asked Questions

Will my spreadsheet fit properly on the page?
Wide spreadsheets are the main challenge, since a sheet that scrolls sideways on screen has to be divided across pages on paper. Setting the print area and page orientation in Excel before converting gives you control over exactly where those breaks fall.
Do multiple worksheets all convert?
Sheets in the workbook are converted in order, each starting on a new page. If you only want one sheet, the cleanest approach is to copy it into a new workbook and convert that, which avoids any ambiguity about what should be included.
Are formulas visible in the PDF?
No — and this is the point of converting. The PDF shows the calculated results as they appear on screen. Recipients see the numbers without the underlying formulas, which is usually exactly what you want when sharing figures.
Will charts and conditional formatting come across?
Yes. Charts, cell colours, conditional formatting, borders, and number formatting all appear in the PDF as they do in Excel. The PDF is a faithful picture of the sheet as it looks on your screen.
Why send a PDF instead of the spreadsheet file?
Because a spreadsheet invites editing and reveals your working. A PDF presents the figures as a finished statement that cannot be accidentally altered, and hides the formulas, hidden columns, and notes that a workbook carries along with it.
Are my files stored on your server?
No. Your workbook is processed in memory and the PDF is streamed straight back to your browser. Nothing is written to disk, nothing is logged, and no copy is retained after your download completes.

Why Convert a Spreadsheet to PDF?

To present results rather than working. A spreadsheet shows how you got there — every formula, every helper column, every note left in a cell during the build. A PDF shows the finished figures. When you are sending numbers to a client, a manager, or a board, the result is what matters and the workings usually are not meant to be part of the conversation.

To prevent accidental changes. Someone reviewing a workbook can type over a value without noticing, and now their copy disagrees with yours with no record of why. A PDF cannot be edited casually.

To guarantee consistent appearance. Spreadsheets render differently depending on version, screen size, and installed fonts. Row heights shift, columns clip, and a carefully arranged sheet can arrive looking broken. A PDF is fixed.

To ensure it can be opened at all. Not everyone has Excel, and mobile devices handle spreadsheet files inconsistently. Every device opens a PDF.

Set the Print Area Before Converting

This is the single most useful piece of preparation, and it takes a minute in Excel.

A spreadsheet has no natural page boundaries — it extends as far as your data goes. A PDF is made of fixed pages. Something has to decide where the divisions fall, and if you do not decide, the converter will.

In Excel, set the print area to the range you actually want, choose landscape orientation for wide tables, and use the scaling option to fit all columns on one page. The Page Break Preview view shows exactly where each division will land, so you can adjust before converting rather than discovering an awkward split afterwards.

Ten minutes spent here is the difference between a PDF that reads as a professional report and one where the last two columns appear alone on a stray final page.

What Carries Across

The PDF reproduces the sheet as it appears on screen. Charts and graphs render at full quality. Cell formatting — colours, borders, fonts, and alignment — is preserved. Conditional formatting appears with its calculated colours applied. Number formatting including currency symbols, decimal places, and date formats displays exactly as configured.

What does not carry across, by design, are the formulas themselves. The PDF shows calculated values, which is precisely the point of the conversion.

Handling Multiple Worksheets

Sheets in a workbook are converted in order, each starting on a new page. If you only need one sheet out of several, copy it into a fresh workbook and convert that — it removes any ambiguity about what gets included and avoids accidentally publishing a working sheet you had forgotten was in the file.

That last point is worth emphasising. Workbooks accumulate hidden sheets, draft calculations, and old versions. Converting the whole file publishes all of it. Checking what is actually in the workbook before converting has saved people from some awkward disclosures.
