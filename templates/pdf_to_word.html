Frequently Asked Questions

Will my layout survive the conversion?
Text-led documents such as reports, letters, and articles convert very well and usually need no cleanup at all. Complex layouts with multiple columns, text boxes, and precisely positioned graphics are harder, because Word has to rebuild in flowing paragraphs what the PDF stored as fixed coordinates. Expect some tidying on design-heavy files.
Can I convert a scanned PDF to Word?
A scan is a photograph of a page, so there is no text layer to convert — you would get pictures inside a Word file. Run the document through Extract Text (OCR) first to recognise the characters, then work with the recognised text.
What file format do I get back?
A standard .docx file, which opens in Microsoft Word, Google Docs, LibreOffice Writer, Apple Pages, and every other modern word processor. There is nothing proprietary about the output.
Will fonts and images be preserved?
Images are carried across into the document. Fonts are matched to what is available on the system opening the file — if the PDF used an unusual typeface that is not installed, Word substitutes the closest match, which can shift line breaks slightly.
Can I convert a password-protected PDF?
Yes. A password prompt appears automatically after upload if the file is encrypted. Enter it once and conversion proceeds normally. Our Unlock PDF tool can also remove the password permanently first.
How do I get back to PDF after editing?
Use Word to PDF. This round trip — PDF to Word, edit, Word to PDF — is the most practical way to genuinely revise the text in a PDF, since editing it in place is severely limited by the format itself.

Why Convert PDF to Word?

Because editing a PDF directly is genuinely difficult, and not by accident. A PDF stores text as individual characters placed at fixed coordinates on a page — that is precisely what makes it look identical on every device. But it means there are no paragraphs to reflow, so changing one sentence in the middle of a page is a structural problem rather than a simple edit.

A Word document stores the same text as flowing paragraphs. Delete a sentence and everything after it moves up automatically. Converting to Word gives you back that flexibility, which is why the PDF → Word → PDF round trip remains the most reliable way to substantially revise a document you only have as a PDF.

What Converts Well and What Needs Cleanup

Being realistic about this saves frustration. Reports, letters, articles, essays, and contracts — anything that is mostly flowing text in a single column — convert extremely well and typically need no correction at all.

Documents with tables usually convert with the table structure intact, though column widths sometimes need adjusting.

Multi-column layouts, newsletters, brochures, and anything designed rather than typed are the hard cases. The converter has to infer reading order and paragraph structure from spatial positions, and where a human eye sees two obvious columns, the underlying data is just characters at coordinates. Expect to spend some time tidying these.

Scanned documents cannot be converted meaningfully at all, because there is no text to convert — only a picture of text. See below.

Scanned PDFs Need OCR First

If your PDF came from a scanner or a photograph, every page is an image. Converting it to Word produces a Word file containing pictures, which is not what anyone wants. The step you need is optical character recognition: Extract Text (OCR) analyses the image and recognises the actual characters, turning the picture into real text you can then edit.

A quick way to check which kind of PDF you have: open it and try to select a sentence with your cursor. If the text highlights, there is a text layer and conversion will work. If nothing selects, it is a scan and you need OCR first.
