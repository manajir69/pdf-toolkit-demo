Frequently Asked Questions

Why convert a presentation to PDF?
Because a .pptx file changes depending on the machine that opens it. Fonts get substituted, text boxes shift, and layouts drift. A PDF is fixed, opens on any device, and cannot be accidentally edited by whoever you send it to.
Does each slide become one page?
Yes. Every slide becomes a page in the same order, so the PDF matches your deck exactly.
Do animations and transitions survive?
No — a PDF is a static format with no concept of timed motion. Each slide appears in its final state, showing all the elements that would have been revealed by animation. Build your deck so each slide reads sensibly in its complete form if you plan to distribute it as a PDF.
Are speaker notes included?
Not in a standard conversion. The PDF contains what appears on the slides themselves. This is usually what you want when sharing — notes are for the presenter rather than the audience.
Will fonts and images look the same?
Yes. Fonts are embedded into the PDF so the deck renders correctly even on a computer that does not have them installed. Images, charts, and shapes keep their appearance and resolution.
Can I combine several presentations into one PDF?
Convert each deck separately, then join the results with Merge PDF. This gives you control over the order of the sections rather than relying on a bulk conversion to guess.

Why Convert a Presentation to PDF?

Because .pptx files do not travel reliably. A PowerPoint file describes a presentation; it does not fix its appearance. Open the same deck on a different computer and the fonts may be substituted, text boxes may resize, bullet spacing may shift, and a slide that was perfectly balanced may arrive with a heading wrapping onto three lines.

This is not a rare edge case. It happens routinely whenever a deck moves between machines with different font sets or PowerPoint versions, and it is why presenters so often arrive to find their slides subtly wrong on the venue's laptop.

A PDF embeds everything. What you see is what every recipient sees, on every device, permanently.

The Other Reasons

Universal access. Not everyone has PowerPoint, and mobile devices handle .pptx inconsistently. Every phone, tablet, and computer opens a PDF without hesitation.

No accidental editing. A .pptx invites changes. Send one to a client and they may edit it, forward the edited version, and create confusion about which deck is current. A PDF cannot be casually altered.

Smaller and simpler. A PDF of a deck is typically much smaller than the .pptx, particularly for image-heavy presentations, which makes it easier to email.

Reliable printing. Handouts print predictably from a PDF, without the layout surprises that come from printing a presentation file on an unfamiliar machine.

Design for the Static Version

A PDF has no animation, so every element that would have appeared in sequence is visible at once. This matters more than it first appears.

A slide built around a progressive reveal — where each bullet arrives as you talk about it — becomes a slide with everything shown simultaneously. If the animation was carrying the meaning, the static version can be confusing or even give away a punchline you meant to build to.

If you know a deck will be distributed as a PDF, design for that. Slides that read clearly in their complete state work in both contexts; slides that depend on timing only work live.

Trainers and educators distributing slides as handouts. Conference speakers submitting decks to organisers. Anyone emailing a large presentation that needs to be smaller.

What Carries Across

Fonts are embedded, so the deck renders correctly even where those typefaces are not installed. This alone solves the most common cause of presentations looking wrong when shared. Images and charts keep their appearance and resolution. Shapes, colours, and gradients are preserved. Slide dimensions — whether 16:9 widescreen or 4:3 — are maintained.

What does not carry across are the elements a static page cannot represent: animations, transitions, embedded video and audio, and speaker notes.
