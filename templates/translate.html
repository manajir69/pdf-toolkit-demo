Frequently Asked Questions

Which languages are supported?
A wide range including Bengali, Arabic, Chinese, Hindi, Spanish, French, German, Portuguese, Russian, Japanese, and many more. Widely spoken languages generally produce better results than less common ones, simply because there is more material behind them.
Can I translate a scanned PDF?
Not directly — a scan contains images rather than text, so there is nothing to translate. Run it through Extract Text (OCR) first to produce a text layer, then translate that.
How accurate is the translation?
Good enough to understand a document's content and act on it in most everyday situations. It is not equivalent to a professional human translation, and should not be relied upon for contracts, legal filings, medical instructions, or anything where a mistranslation carries real consequences.
Will the layout be preserved?
Translated text is often longer or shorter than the original — German runs noticeably longer than English, for instance — so line breaks and spacing shift. The content is the focus rather than an exact visual reproduction.
Does it handle right-to-left languages?
Arabic, Hebrew, Urdu, and other right-to-left scripts are supported, though the layout may require adjustment since the reading direction differs from the original document's structure.
Are my documents stored on your server?
No. Your file is processed in memory and discarded once the translation is complete. Nothing is written to disk, nothing is logged, and no copy is retained.

Why Translating a Whole Document Matters

Copying paragraphs one at a time into a translation box is workable for a sentence and unbearable for a forty-page report. You lose your place, the context between sections disappears, and by page ten the effort exceeds any value you were getting from the document.

Translating the document as a unit keeps the structure intact and lets you read it the way it was meant to be read — start to finish, with headings and sections in place.

What This Is Genuinely Useful For

Understanding correspondence. A letter from a foreign supplier, a notice from a government office, an invoice from an overseas vendor — you need to know what it says before you can respond.

Research across languages. Relevant material is frequently published in languages you do not read. Translation makes it accessible enough to determine whether it matters to your work.

Technical documentation. Manuals, specifications, and instructions for equipment sourced internationally.

Reading widely. Reports, articles, and publications from other countries that would otherwise be closed to you.

Where Machine Translation Falls Short

Being clear about the limits makes the tool more useful rather than less, because you know when to trust it.

Machine translation handles straightforward informational prose well. A technical description, a factual report, a business letter — these convey their meaning accurately enough to act on.

It struggles with idiom and cultural reference, which often translate into literal nonsense. It struggles with legal and contractual language, where precise terms carry defined meanings that do not map cleanly between jurisdictions. It struggles with ambiguity, where a human translator would use context to choose the right reading and a machine picks the statistically likely one, which is sometimes wrong.

Do Not Rely on It for Consequential Documents

This warrants its own emphasis. Contracts, legal filings, medical instructions, safety documentation, and regulatory submissions require professional human translation.

The failure mode is what makes this important: machine translation does not produce obvious gibberish when it goes wrong. It produces fluent, confident text that means something different from the original. You cannot tell by reading it that a clause has been inverted or a dosage misstated, because the translation reads perfectly well.

Use machine translation to understand what a document is about. Use a professional translator for anything you will sign, submit, or act on medically.

Layout Will Shift

Translated text is rarely the same length as the original. German typically runs longer than English; Chinese is usually much shorter. When the text length changes, line breaks and spacing necessarily change with it, so the translated document will not be a visual match for the original. The content is what is being carried across, not the exact page layout.

How to Translate a PDF

Upload your PDF by dragging it onto the box or clicking to browse. Choose the target language. The translation runs and the result is returned for you to read. Longer documents take proportionally more time.

Scanned Documents Need OCR First

A scanned PDF contains images of text rather than text itself, so there is nothing to translate. Extract Text (OCR) recognises the characters first, after which translation works normally. Note that OCR errors carry through into the translation, so scan quality affects the final result more than people expect.

Your Privacy Is Protected

Your document is processed in memory and discarded once the translation completes. Nothing is written to disk, nothing is logged, and no copy is retained.
