Frequently Asked Questions

What kind of pages convert well?
Article-style pages — news stories, blog posts, documentation, and reference material — convert well because the content is mostly text in a clear reading order. Heavily interactive pages, dashboards, and applications convert poorly, since there is no static version of them to capture.
Why does the PDF look different from the web page?
Because the content is extracted and reformatted for print rather than photographed. Navigation menus, sidebars, adverts, and cookie banners are stripped out, leaving the actual article. The result reads well on paper even though it does not look like a screenshot.
Can I convert a page that requires logging in?
No. The converter fetches the page as an anonymous visitor, so anything behind a login, a paywall, or a members-only area is not accessible. Only publicly reachable pages can be converted.
Why does JavaScript-heavy content go missing?
The page is fetched as delivered by the server. Content that a browser would load afterwards by running scripts is not present at that moment, so single-page applications and infinitely scrolling feeds often produce very little.
Is it legal to save a web page as a PDF?
Saving a copy for personal reading, research, or reference is ordinary practice. Republishing or redistributing someone else's content is a different matter and depends on their copyright and licensing. Use the tool for your own reference.
What is this most useful for?
Archiving. Web pages change and disappear, and a PDF captures what a page said on the day you read it — useful for research citations, keeping records of terms and prices, and saving reference material for offline reading.

Why Save a Web Page as a PDF?

Because the web is not permanent. Pages are edited, moved, paywalled, and deleted. A URL that works today may return nothing in two years, and the content you relied on is simply gone. A PDF captures what the page said on the day you saved it.

This matters most for research, where a citation needs to point at something that still exists, and for records — the terms and conditions you agreed to, the price you were quoted, the specification you ordered against. Web pages change quietly and without notice; a saved PDF does not.

Offline reading is the other common reason. Long articles saved as PDFs can be read on a flight, on a train, or anywhere the connection is poor, on any device.

How the Conversion Works

The page is fetched and its main content is extracted — the actual article text, headings, and structure — while the surrounding page furniture is discarded. Navigation menus, sidebars, adverts, cookie banners, social sharing widgets, comment sections, and related-article panels all go.

The result is a clean, readable document rather than a screenshot. This is a deliberate design choice: a photograph of a web page includes all the clutter and reads badly on paper, while extracted content flows properly across pages and prints legibly.

The consequence is that the PDF will not look like the web page. It contains the same words, laid out for reading rather than for browsing.

What Converts Well and What Does Not

Articles and blog posts convert excellently. The content is text in a clear reading order, which is exactly what the extraction is designed for.

Documentation and reference pages convert well, including their headings and code blocks.

News stories convert well, though some sites detect automated fetching and serve a reduced version.

Single-page applications and dashboards convert poorly. Their content is assembled in the browser by JavaScript after the initial page loads, so a server fetch sees very little.

Anything behind a login or paywall cannot be converted at all, since the fetch is made as an anonymous visitor.

Infinite-scroll feeds yield only whatever loaded initially, which is typically a small fraction of what you would see after scrolling.

The distinction is between keeping a copy and passing it off or passing it on — use the tool for the former.
